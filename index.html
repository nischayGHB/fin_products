§1 — Foundation

Market Architecture & Taxonomy

Financial markets are not monolithic. They are a collection of distinct ecosystems — each with its own price discovery mechanism, clearing infrastructure, participant set, liquidity regime, and risk topology. Understanding the architecture before the instruments is mandatory.

The Market Taxonomy

Financial markets partition along three primary axes: venue type (exchange vs. OTC), instrument class (spot, derivative, structured), and settlement mechanism (physical vs. cash, T+0 to T+2 to long-dated). These axes combine to create dozens of distinct market structures with materially different risk, liquidity, and operational characteristics.

Exchange-Traded
Centralized Price Discovery
Standardized contracts, central limit order books (CLOB), central clearing via CCP. Price is public and continuous. Examples: equity shares, listed futures, listed options. Margin is segregated via CCP rulebook.
OTC Markets
Bilateral Negotiation
Bespoke terms, bilateral credit risk (or cleared via CCP post-Dodd-Frank/EMIR). Price is discovered via dealer quotes (RFQ). Examples: IRS, CDS, FX forwards, structured products. ISDA framework governs legal relationships.
Dark & Alternative
Off-Exchange Execution
Dark pools, crossing networks, internalization. No pre-trade transparency. Used by institutions to minimize market impact. Subject to best execution rules (MiFID II, Reg NMS). ~35-40% of US equity volume is off-exchange.
Primary Markets
Capital Formation
IPOs, bond issuance, secondary offerings, rights issues. Price is set via bookbuilding or Dutch auction. Underwriters (investment banks) bridge issuers and institutional investors. The only moment of true "new money" entering a security.

Clearing & Settlement Architecture

Every trade has a pre-settlement lifecycle. Execution is the visible part; clearing and settlement are the invisible plumbing that determine whether a trade actually occurs. Failures here are systemic.

Key Concept

Central Counterparty (CCP): Upon trade execution, the CCP novates itself between buyer and seller. Each party now has a contract with the CCP — not with each other. This eliminates bilateral counterparty risk but concentrates it at the CCP (too-big-to-fail risk). CCPs are the single most systemically important entities in modern markets. Margin Architecture

Two margin types exist: Initial Margin (IM) — pre-funded against potential future exposure — and Variation Margin (VM) — daily settlement of mark-to-market. CCP margin models (SPAN, VaR-based, ISDA SIMM for bilateral) determine IM requirements using historical simulation or parametric VaR on a 99% confidence, typically 5-day holding period basis. Pro-cyclicality is a known model risk: IM spikes during stress, forcing deleveraging when liquidity is worst.

Prime Brokerage Mechanics

Prime brokers are the life support of the hedge fund ecosystem. They provide: (1) Securities financing — repo and securities lending to create leverage; (2) Custody — assets held in prime's name (rehypothecation risk); (3) Margin — portfolio-level margin using cross-margining and VaR-based systems; (4) Capital introduction — connecting HFs with investors; (5) Operational support — trade confirmation, reconciliation, reporting.

Risk Alert

Rehypothecation: Prime brokers can re-use client collateral for their own financing purposes (up to 140% of client debit in the US under Rule 15c3-3; unlimited in the UK). This creates a hidden leverage loop. When a prime broker fails (Lehman 2008), clients' re-hypothecated assets are in the estate — not returned automatically. MF Global 2011 showed the catastrophic version with commingled segregated client funds.

§3 — Asset Class

Equity Markets

Equities represent residual claims on corporate cash flows after all other claimants are satisfied. Their value is a function of expected future earnings, discount rates, and the multiple the market assigns to those earnings — each of which is itself a function of complex macro and micro dynamics.

Equity Valuation Frameworks

Dividend Discount Model (DDM)

Gordon Growth Model (constant growth DDM)
P₀ = D₁ / (r − g)

where: D₁ = expected dividend next period | r = required return on equity | g = perpetual growth rate

The DDM is the theoretical foundation but is practically limited: most growth companies pay no dividends, g is unstable, and small errors in (r-g) cause massive valuation swings. It is most applicable to mature, dividend-paying utilities, REITs, and regulated industries.

DCF / Free Cash Flow

Discounted Free Cash Flow to Equity
P₀ = Σ [FCFEₜ / (1+r)ᵗ] + [Terminal Value / (1+r)ⁿ]

FCFE = Net Income − ΔNet Working Capital − CapEx + Depreciation + Net Borrowing

Terminal Value = FCFEₙ₊₁ / (r − g) [Gordon perpetuity]
Key sensitivities: WACC (discount rate composition) | Terminal growth rate | Forecast horizon length

Institutional Note

Multiple expansion vs. earnings growth: Equity returns decompose into: (1) earnings growth, (2) dividend yield, and (3) multiple change (P/E expansion or compression). During 2010-2021, a significant portion of US equity returns came from multiple expansion driven by falling discount rates. As rates rise, multiple compression becomes the dominant headwind even if EPS grows. This is the central tension in 2023-2025 equity positioning.

Short Selling Mechanics

Short selling is the sale of borrowed securities. The mechanism: (1) Locate shares from a securities lender (custodian bank, long-only fund). (2) Borrow against collateral (cash or bonds) posted to the lender. (3) Sell the shares in the market. (4) Cover by buying back at a later date. (5) Return shares to lender.

Short Selling P&L
P&L = (Sale Price − Cover Price) × Shares − Borrow Cost − Dividend Obligations

Borrow Cost (annualized) = Borrow Rate × Days Held / 360

Borrow Rate ranges from 25bps (GC/easy-to-borrow) to 20%+ (specials/HTB stocks)
Risk: Short Squeeze

A short squeeze occurs when: (1) a heavily shorted stock rises, (2) short sellers face margin calls, (3) forced covering amplifies the move, (4) lenders recall shares (buy-in risk), creating a feedback loop. Short interest as % of float, days-to-cover (short interest ÷ ADV), and borrow availability are the key metrics. GME January 2021 was the canonical modern short squeeze — short interest exceeded 100% of float (via rehypothecation), with days-to-cover of 30+.

Microstructure is the study of how trades actually happen — the mechanics of price discovery, order flow, spread formation, adverse selection, and the strategic behavior of market participants around a trade. This determines real execution costs, which in any high-frequency or large-scale strategy dominate all other considerations.

Spread Decomposition

The bid-ask spread is not random — it decomposes into three economically distinct components, each managed differently by market makers:

Glosten-Milgrom Spread Decomposition
Spread = Adverse Selection Component + Inventory Cost + Order Processing Cost

Adverse Selection = 2 × α × (V − P)
where α = probability counterparty is informed, V = true value, P = current price

Market makers quote wide spreads when: α is high (earnings, news imminent) | inventory is extreme | volatility spikes

Adverse selection is the core problem of market making: informed traders know more than the market maker and always trade against the maker's interest. The maker recovers this loss by charging uninformed traders (hedgers, noise traders) via the spread. HFT with faster data feeds can identify informed flow earlier, forcing traditional market makers to widen spreads further.

Transaction Cost Analysis (TCA)

Implementation Shortfall
IS = (Realized Price − Decision Price) / Decision Price × Position Size

IS decomposes into:
├── Delay Cost: decision-to-first-trade price move
├── Market Impact: price move caused by own trading
├── Timing Risk: price volatility during execution
└── Opportunity Cost: unexecuted portion × price move
Institutional Reality

Market impact models: Almgren-Chriss (2000) remains the canonical framework. Market impact is roughly: MI ≈ σ × (Q/V)^0.5 × f(speed), where σ is volatility, Q is order size, V is average daily volume, and f(speed) is an increasing function of trading urgency. Temporary impact decays; permanent impact (the information component) does not. For large institutional orders (>5% of ADV), impact dominates all other costs. The optimal trade schedule balances impact (lower with slow trading) against timing risk (lower with fast trading).

§5 — Equity Products

ETFs & Index Products

ETFs are the most consequential financial innovation of the last 30 years by market structure impact. They combine the diversification of mutual funds with intraday tradability — and introduce a complex creation/redemption arbitrage mechanism that links ETF prices to their NAV in a non-trivial way.

The Creation/Redemption Mechanism

The ETF premium/discount to NAV is kept tight (typically 1-5bps in liquid equity ETFs) by Authorized Participants (APs) — typically large broker-dealers — who can create or redeem ETF shares in kind against the underlying basket.

AP assembles basket of underlying stocks
→
Delivers to ETF sponsor in exchange for new ETF shares
→
AP sells ETF shares in secondary market at premium

Redemption works in reverse: AP buys ETF shares (at a discount to NAV), delivers to sponsor, receives the basket. This mechanism creates a powerful arbitrage that eliminates persistent premiums/discounts — unless the underlying basket is illiquid, crosses multiple time zones, or involves restrictions on short-selling.

Risk: ETF Stress Scenarios

Fixed income ETF breakdown: During March 2020, investment-grade bond ETFs (LQD, VCIT) traded at discounts of 3-5% to NAV for several days. The underlying corporate bonds were illiquid (2-3 market makers, wide bid-ask spreads, limited institutional supply). APs could not construct redemption baskets efficiently. The ETF became the price-discovery vehicle for the underlying market — a structural reversal of the normal relationship. This created opportunities for sophisticated traders but highlighted the NAV-price gap risk during dislocations.

Synthetic ETFs

Synthetic ETFs (common in Europe) do not hold the underlying basket. Instead, they hold a substitute basket (often unrelated assets) as collateral and enter into a total return swap with a dealer who delivers the index return. This introduces: (1) counterparty risk to the swap dealer, (2) collateral quality risk, (3) securities lending income sharing complexity. UCITS rules cap unfunded swap exposure at 10% of NAV, providing some protection.

Index Construction & Rebalancing Mechanics

Index rebalancing creates predictable, front-runnable order flow. When a stock is added to the S&P 500, passive funds holding ~$7 trillion of S&P 500 products must buy the stock simultaneously. Academic research (Greenwood 2008, Kaul et al.) documents 3-7% price increases at announcement with partial reversal — a pure mechanical flow effect unrelated to fundamentals.

Strategy Note

Index arbitrage and gamma effects: The S&P 500 reconstitution trade is one of the longest-running systematic strategies. Add candidates identified via index methodology rules (market cap, liquidity, profitability screens) can be bought on announcement date (typically 1-3 weeks before effective date) and sold on or just before the effective date when passive flow peaks. Execution timing relative to the official close of the effective date is critical — passive funds replicate exactly at the closing auction.

§6 — Fixed Income

Bond Mechanics & Pricing

A bond is a contractual obligation to pay specified cash flows at specified times. The apparent simplicity hides enormous complexity in how bonds are priced, how their risk is measured, and how they interact with credit, rates, and liquidity dimensions.

Bond Price / Yield Relationship

Bond Pricing — Full Price (Dirty Price)
P = Σ [C / (1+y/m)^(t×m)] + [FV / (1+y/m)^(n×m)]

where t = 1 to n (periods), C = coupon payment, y = yield to maturity, m = coupons/year, FV = face value

Clean Price = Dirty Price − Accrued Interest
Accrued Interest = C × (Days since last coupon / Days in coupon period)
Yield to Maturity is the single discount rate that equates PV of all cash flows to market price — it is an internal rate of return assumption, not a forward rate forecast

Bond price and yield move inversely. This is not merely a mathematical relationship — it is the central tension in fixed income portfolio management. A long bond portfolio is short duration: any rise in rates creates mark-to-market losses. The magnitude of these losses is captured by duration and convexity.

Duration

Macaulay Duration, Modified Duration, DV01
Macaulay Duration = Σ [t × PV(CFₜ)] / P
(cash-flow-weighted average time to receipt)

Modified Duration = Macaulay Duration / (1 + y/m)

ΔP/P ≈ −ModDur × Δy (first-order approximation)

DV01 = −(ΔP / Δy) = ModDur × P × 0.0001
DV01 is price change per 1 basis point (0.01%) move in yield
A 10-year Treasury with ModDur = 9.0 loses ~9% in price for a 100bps rise in yield

Convexity

Second-Order Price Approximation with Convexity
ΔP/P ≈ −ModDur × Δy + ½ × Convexity × (Δy)²

Convexity = [1 / (P × (1+y)²)] × Σ [t(t+1) × CFₜ / (1+y)ᵗ]
Positive convexity: price rises more than duration predicts when yields fall; falls less when yields rise → always good for longs
Negative convexity: callable bonds, mortgage-backed securities — duration extends when rates rise (prepayments slow), shortens when rates fall (prepayments accelerate)

§7 — Fixed Income

Rates & Yield Curves

The yield curve is simultaneously a monetary policy signal, a macro forecast, a credit benchmark, a valuation tool, and a trading opportunity. Understanding all of its dimensions — shape, level, curvature, richness/cheapness — is foundational to fixed income, derivatives, equity, and FX positioning.

Yield Curve Theories

Pure Expectations

  • Long rates = average of expected future short rates
  • No term premium
  • Implication: upward slope → rising rate expectations
  • Fails empirically: term premium exists

Liquidity Preference

  • Long rates = expected short rates + liquidity premium
  • Premium rises with maturity (investors demand compensation for duration risk)
  • Explains why curve is typically upward-sloping even in flat rate environments

Market Segmentation

  • Different investors have preferred habitats (pension funds → long end)
  • Supply/demand in each maturity segment independently drives rates
  • Explains why QE (buying long end) flattens the curve regardless of short rate expectations

Key Rate Duration (KRD)

Rather than summarizing interest rate risk as a single duration number, KRD measures sensitivity to changes at each maturity bucket (2Y, 5Y, 10Y, 30Y etc.) independently. This allows precise hedging of non-parallel curve moves. A portfolio with 50% of KRD at the 30Y point behaves very differently from one concentrated at 2Y — even if modified duration is identical.

Rate Benchmarks: LIBOR Transition

The LIBOR scandal (2012) and subsequent replacement with risk-free rates (RFRs) represents the largest structural change in rates markets in decades. LIBOR was a term rate with a credit component (it reflected interbank borrowing costs). Its replacements are overnight rates based on actual transactions — no credit component, no term premium.

SOFR vs. LIBOR basis: Legacy contracts (loans, bonds, swaps) that referenced LIBOR were either renegotiated or converted via fallback language (ISDA protocol). §8 — Fixed Income

Credit Markets & Spread Products

Credit investing is the business of lending money at a spread over risk-free rates, and being compensated for the risk that the borrower defaults. The spread must cover expected default losses, the uncertainty around them, and an illiquidity premium. Getting this decomposition right is the entirety of credit analysis.

Spread Measures

Credit Spread Measures — From Simple to Sophisticated
Nominal Spread = YTM(bond) − YTM(benchmark Treasury of similar maturity)
[Ignores curve shape; crude but fast]

P = Σ [CFₜ / (1 + zₜ + Z)ᵗ] where zₜ = risk-free zero rate at time t
[Accounts for curve shape; assumes no embedded options]

OAS (Option-Adjusted Spread) = Z-Spread − Option Cost
[For callable bonds/MBS: strips out the option value before measuring credit premium]
Estimated via Monte Carlo across interest rate paths

ASW (Asset Swap Spread) = bond yield − IBOR/SOFR swap rate
[Used in the swap market; measures bond richness vs. swaps]

Key Concept — Recovery Rate

Default alone does not define loss. Expected Loss = PD × (1 − Recovery Rate). Recovery rates for senior secured debt average 60-70%, senior unsecured 40-50%, subordinated 20-30%, equity near zero in bankruptcy. Recoveries vary enormously by industry: capital-intensive sectors (utilities, airlines) have tangible assets; software/financials may recover very little. The recovery rate is a key input to CDS pricing and CLO modeling.

§9 — Money Markets

Repo & Money Markets

The repo market is the circulatory system of the financial system. It is where banks, dealers, hedge funds, and central banks finance their securities holdings on a short-term (overnight to 3-month) basis. Without repo, modern leveraged finance does not function.

Repo Mechanics

A repurchase agreement (repo) is economically equivalent to a collateralized loan. The seller/borrower sells securities to the buyer/lender with an agreement to repurchase them at a higher price (the difference reflects interest). The buyer holds collateral as protection against default.

Repo Mechanics
Cash Received = Market Value of Collateral × (1 − Haircut)

Repurchase Price = Cash Lent × (1 + Repo Rate × Days/360)

Implied Repo Rate = [(Repurchase Price/Purchase Price) − 1] × (360/Days)

Haircut = over-collateralization. On-the-run Treasuries: 0-2%. IG corporate bonds: 5-10%. HY bonds: 15-25%. Equities: 10-20%. The haircut absorbs potential value decline during close-out.

General Collateral (GC) vs. Specials

Most repo uses GC collateral — any acceptable security from a broad pool. The GC repo rate tracks closely to central bank policy rates. Specific securities in high demand (e.g., on-the-run Treasury being squeezed in the futures delivery mechanism) trade in repo at a rate significantly below GC — these are called specials. When a security trades very special (repo rate near zero or even negative), it signals acute short interest or forced covering.

Systemic Risk

Repo runs: The 2008 financial crisis was fundamentally a repo run. When Lehman Brothers' solvency was questioned, repo counterparties refused to roll their overnight repos, requiring Lehman to sell assets into an illiquid market. Gorton and Metrick (2012) document "haircut spirals" — as collateral values fell, lenders demanded higher haircuts, forcing more asset sales, further depressing prices. The tri-party repo market (JPM and BNY Mellon as clearing banks) saw $2.8 trillion of intraday credit evaporate in hours. SOFR was designed to measure and anchor this market.

Reverse Repo & Fed Operations

The Federal Reserve's Overnight Reverse Repo (ON RRP) facility allows eligible counterparties (money market funds, primary dealers, GSEs) to deposit cash with the Fed overnight in exchange for Treasuries. This sets a floor on money market rates. During 2022-2023, ON RRP balances exceeded $2.5 trillion — a sign of excess reserves and the Fed's QT program working to mop up surplus liquidity.

§10 — Derivatives

Futures & Forwards

Futures and forwards are agreements to transact at a predetermined price at a future date. They differ in standardization, venue, and settlement — but share the same fundamental pricing logic anchored to spot price plus cost of carry.

Cost of Carry Pricing

Forward/Futures Pricing — Cost of Carry Model
F = S × e^(r−q)T [continuous compounding]
F = S × (1 + r − q)^T [discrete compounding]

For commodities with storage costs (u) and convenience yield (y):
F = S × e^(r + u − y)T

S = spot price | r = risk-free rate | q = dividend/income yield | T = time to expiry (years)
u = storage cost rate | y = convenience yield (benefit of holding physical commodity)

The Basis

Basis = Spot − Futures (or Futures − Spot depending on convention). In a normal world, basis converges to zero at expiry. Deviations from theoretical forward prices create basis that can be arbitraged — but not costlessly. Real-world frictions (financing costs, borrow costs, transaction costs, balance sheet constraints) mean basis can persist and even widen.

Convexity Adjustment (Futures vs. Forwards)

Interest rate futures (Eurodollar/SOFR) are not equivalent to FRAs (forward rate agreements) of the same maturity. Because futures are marked to market daily, the daily P&L correlation with rates creates a convexity bias. Forward Rate = Futures Rate − ½ × σ² × T₁ × T₂ where σ is rate volatility, T₁ is time to futures expiry, T₂ is time to end of reference period. This adjustment grows with maturity — for a 5-year SOFR futures strip, the convexity adjustment can be 15-30bps at the far end.

Options Theory

Options are contracts that grant the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset at a specific price on or before a specific date. They are nonlinear instruments — their value is a function of price, volatility, time, and rates — and this nonlinearity is simultaneously what makes them powerful hedging tools and complex risk management challenges.

Call: C = S × e^(−qT) × N(d₁) − K × e^(−rT) × N(d₂)

Put-Call Parity: C − P = S × e^(−qT) − K × e^(−rT)
S = spot | K = strike | r = risk-free rate | q = dividend yield | σ = implied volatility | T = time to expiry | N(·) = standard normal CDF

Gamma-Theta Tradeoff — The Core Option P&L Equation

For a delta-hedged option position, the daily P&L is approximately:

Daily P&L ≈ ½ × Γ × (ΔS)² − Θ × Δt

This is the fundamental tradeoff: you pay theta daily for the right to benefit when the underlying moves by more than expected. If realized volatility exceeds implied volatility, long gamma wins. If realized vol is below implied, short gamma (theta collecting) wins. The entire options market is a continuous auction for this bet.

§13 — Derivatives

The Volatility Surface

In BSM, a single volatility parameter generates option prices. In reality, options of different strikes and maturities imply different volatilities when BSM is inverted. This map of implied vol across strike and maturity is the volatility surface — a three-dimensional representation of market pricing that captures distributional assumptions, supply/demand dynamics, and hedging costs.

Skew & Smile

Volatility skew (equity markets): OTM puts trade at higher implied vol than OTM calls at the same distance from the money. This reflects: (1) demand for tail protection/insurance, (2) realized negative return skew (markets fall faster than they rise), (3) leverage effect (as prices fall, volatility rises). The 25-delta risk reversal (RR) = IV(25Δ Put) − IV(25Δ Call) quantifies skew.

Volatility smile (FX, some commodity markets): Both OTM puts and calls trade at higher IV than ATM options, creating a U-shape. The 25-delta butterfly (BF) = ½ × [IV(25Δ Put) + IV(25Δ Call)] − IV(ATM) quantifies this smile premium.

Term Structure of Volatility

The vol term structure is typically upward-sloping (long-dated options more expensive) because uncertainty compounds over time. This flattens or inverts when short-term vol spikes (event, crisis). VIX futures term structure is typically in contango — forward vol > spot vol — creating a persistent negative roll yield for long VIX ETPs (e.g., VXX historically lost 60-80%/year in calm markets).

§15 — Swaps & OTC

Interest Rate Swaps

The interest rate swap (IRS) market is the largest derivatives market in the world by notional — exceeding $500 trillion. A vanilla IRS exchanges a fixed rate for a floating rate on a notional principal. Pricing, risk management, and usage cut across every institutional sector.

IRS Mechanics & Pricing

In a plain vanilla IRS, one party pays a fixed rate (the swap rate) and receives a floating rate (SOFR, SONIA, €STR), or vice versa, on a notional principal over an agreed tenor. No notional is exchanged. Payments are netted on settlement dates (typically quarterly or semi-annually).

IRS Pricing — Par Swap Rate
At inception, a fairly-priced swap has zero market value (NPV = 0):

Fixed Leg PV = Σ [c × τᵢ × DF(tᵢ)]
Floating Leg PV = Σ [f(tᵢ₋₁,tᵢ) × τᵢ × DF(tᵢ)] ≈ 1 − DF(tₙ) [for LIBOR/SOFR strips]

Par Swap Rate: c = [1 − DF(tₙ)] / Σ [τᵢ × DF(tᵢ)]

DF(t) = discount factor for time t | τᵢ = day count fraction for period i | f = forward rate

DV01 of a Swap

A receiver swap (receive fixed, pay floating) has positive duration — gains when rates fall. A payer swap (pay fixed, receive floating) has negative duration — gains when rates rise. DV01 of a 10Y swap ≈ $900 per $1M notional per basis point. This makes swaps extremely capital-efficient for duration management — a pension fund can add $100M notional of 30Y receiver swaps for a fraction of the cost of buying $100M of 30Y bonds.

Swap Curve vs. Treasury Curve

The swap spread (swap rate minus equivalent maturity Treasury yield) reflects: (1) bank credit risk (pre-crisis positive), (2) supply/demand for fixed receivers from pensions/insurance, (3) balance sheet constraints on dealers to intermediate. Post-2008, US 30Y swap spreads turned negative — meaning 30Y swap rates fell below Treasury yields — due to heavy receiving from pension funds and dealer constraints. This was a structural, not transient, phenomenon.

Credit Default Swaps

A Credit Default Swap (CDS) is an insurance contract on credit risk. The protection buyer pays a periodic premium (the CDS spread, in basis points per annum) and receives a payment if the reference entity experiences a credit event (default, restructuring, failure to pay). CDS allow credit risk to be traded independently of interest rate risk.

§17 — Swaps & OTC

Cross-Currency Swaps & Basis

Cross-currency swaps exchange both interest payments and principal in two different currencies. They are the primary instrument for long-term FX hedging and reveal the cross-currency basis — the deviation from covered interest rate parity that reflects funding cost differentials between currencies.

Cross-Currency Basis & Covered Interest Rate Parity

Covered Interest Rate Parity (CIP) — The Theoretical Anchor
F/S = (1 + r_domestic) / (1 + r_foreign) [discrete]
ln(F/S) = r_domestic − r_foreign [continuous]

CIP states: forward premium = interest rate differential
If violated, riskless arbitrage profit exists

Cross-Currency Basis (xccy basis) = Deviation from CIP
Observed: Forward Rate_mkt = S × (1 + r_d + basis_d) / (1 + r_f + basis_f)

Negative USD xccy basis (e.g., EUR/USD xccy basis = −30bps) means: borrowing USD via FX swap costs 30bps more than direct USD borrowing. This is structural, not arbitrage.

CIP should hold in theory — if it doesn't, a riskless arbitrage exists. In practice, CIP violations (cross-currency basis) persist because the "arbitrage" requires: (1) balance sheet capacity (costly post-Basel III), (2) counterparty credit lines, (3) regulatory capital. USD demand from non-US banks (European, Japanese) borrowing dollars via FX swaps creates persistent negative basis — meaning the dollar is "expensive" on the swap market.

Institutional Use

Japanese life insurers and USD basis: JGBs yield near zero; USD Treasuries yield 4-5%. Japanese institutions buy USD Treasuries and hedge the FX exposure via USD/JPY cross-currency swaps. The hedging cost (USD xccy basis + interest rate differential cost) determines whether the trade is profitable. When USD basis is very negative (−100bps), the fully-hedged USD Treasury yield can be near or below JGB yields — making the trade uneconomical and forcing either unhedged exposure or repatriation. This is a major driver of capital flow dynamics between Japan and the US.

§18 — Swaps & OTC

OTC Infrastructure: ISDA, CSA & Clearing

OTC derivative contracts exist within a legal, operational, and collateral infrastructure that is as important as the instrument itself. The ISDA Master Agreement, Credit Support Annex, and post-GFC clearing mandates define the actual risk of any OTC transaction.

ISDA Framework

The ISDA Master Agreement is the foundational legal document governing all OTC derivative transactions between two counterparties. Key provisions: (1) Netting — in a default, all transactions under the agreement are collapsed into a single net obligation, reducing counterparty exposure dramatically; (2) Close-out netting — upon default, the non-defaulting party can terminate all trades and calculate a single net payment; (3) Events of Default and Termination Events — defines what triggers close-out.

The Credit Support Annex (CSA) governs collateral posting between parties. Key CSA terms: (1) Eligible collateral — what can be posted (cash, Treasuries, agency bonds; haircuts apply); (2) Threshold — amount of uncollateralized exposure permitted before margin calls begin (typically $0 for financial institutions post-GFC); (3) MTA (Minimum Transfer Amount) — smallest collateral movement required; (4) ISDA SIMM — the standard model for initial margin in bilateral (non-cleared) swaps under BCBS-IOSCO UMR rules.

Clearing Mandate (Dodd-Frank / EMIR)

Post-2008, regulators mandated CCP clearing for standardized OTC derivatives (vanilla IRS, index CDS) to reduce systemic bilateral counterparty risk. Bespoke, illiquid, or custom-tailored trades remain bilateral. The clearing mandate created a two-tier OTC market: cleared (lower counterparty risk, margin required, standardized) and uncleared (bilateral, higher capital charge, SIMM margin under UMR).

§19 — Foreign Exchange

FX Spot & Forwards

FX is the largest and most liquid market in the world — $7.5 trillion per day in 2022 BIS data. It is entirely OTC, runs 24/5 across global time zones, and involves a complex ecosystem of dealers, ECNs, central banks, and end-users. FX prices every cross-border transaction, investment, and trade flow.

§20 — Foreign Exchange

Carry Trades & Non-Deliverable Forwards

Carry trades exploit interest rate differentials across currencies. Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) allow forward FX hedging in currencies where physical delivery or free FX markets are restricted.

FX Carry Trade

The carry trade borrows in low-yielding currencies (JPY, CHF) and invests in high-yielding currencies (MXN, BRL, TRY historically). Under Uncovered Interest Rate Parity (UIP), higher-yielding currencies should depreciate to offset the interest differential. UIP fails empirically — the "forward premium puzzle" — high-yield currencies tend to appreciate in calm markets, generating carry profits. However, carry unwinds violently in risk-off environments.

Carry Trade Return Decomposition
Return = Interest Differential − FX Depreciation

Expected Return (Carry) = r_high − r_low [if UIP fails, exchange rate doesn't move to offset]

Risk: "Carry crashes" — AUD/JPY lost 35% in 6 weeks in 2008; TRY and BRL periodically collapse

Sharpe Ratio of carry ≈ 0.4-0.7 (positive but skewed: many small gains, rare large losses)

Non-Deliverable Forwards (NDFs)

NDFs are FX forward contracts where settlement is in USD (or another convertible currency) rather than the restricted currency. At maturity, the difference between the contracted forward rate and the official fixing rate is settled in cash. Used for: CNY (offshore USD/CNH is deliverable but onshore USD/CNY is not), INR, BRL, KRW, TWD, IDR, PHP, CLP.

Institutional Note

Onshore vs offshore spread: The USD/CNH (offshore deliverable) vs. USD/CNY (onshore fixing) spread reveals capital flow pressures and PBOC policy intentions. When CNH trades weaker than CNY, it signals outflow pressure or expectation of CNY depreciation. The PBOC manages the onshore rate via a daily fixing mechanism with a ±2% band; offshore rates can diverge, creating a two-speed market that institutional investors monitor closely.

Commodity Markets

Commodity markets sit at the intersection of physical supply/demand and financial speculation. Unlike financial assets, commodities have storage costs, convenience yields, physical delivery constraints, and seasonal patterns. The futures curve shape (contango vs. backwardation) is the central signal of physical market conditions.

Commodity Forward Curve Dynamics
Futures Price: F(t,T) = S(t) × e^[(r + u − y)(T−t)]

where: u = storage cost, y = convenience yield, r = financing rate

CONTANGO: F > S (futures above spot) → u > y (storage exceeds convenience yield)
Signals: ample supply, low immediate demand, markets well-supplied

BACKWARDATION: F < S (futures below spot) → y > u (convenience yield exceeds storage cost)
Signals: tight physical supply, strong immediate demand, inventory squeeze

In contango: rolling involves selling low (near), buying high (far) → negative roll yield
In backwardation: rolling earns positive yield → total return > spot return

§22 — Commodities

Energy Markets & Curve Dynamics

Energy markets — oil, gas, power — are among the most complex in financial markets. They are simultaneously physical logistics problems and speculative vehicles. The April 2020 WTI negative price event demonstrated that when physical reality (Cushing storage exhaustion) meets financial position (passive ETF rolling), extraordinary dislocations occur.

Oil Market Structure

Brent Crude (ICE) is the global benchmark — priced at a North Sea delivery point, with cash settlement option. It governs ~70% of global oil trade pricing. WTI (NYMEX/CME) is the US benchmark — physically delivered at Cushing, Oklahoma. The Brent-WTI spread reflects: US export capacity, Cushing storage levels, pipeline infrastructure, and US domestic demand/production dynamics. During the US shale boom (2012-2016), WTI traded at a $5-15 discount to Brent due to inland Cushing bottlenecks.

Natural Gas — Extreme Seasonality & Regional Fragmentation

Natural gas markets are highly regional (unlike oil, gas cannot easily move by tanker in non-LNG form). Henry Hub (NYMEX) is the US benchmark. TTF (Title Transfer Facility) is the European benchmark. These markets can diverge massively: in 2022, European TTF gas hit €340/MWh (equivalent to ~$500/barrel oil) post-Ukraine invasion, while US Henry Hub remained near $8-9/MMBtu. The price signal was correct — it rationed European demand and attracted US LNG exports. The spread between US and European prices is an arbitrage constrained by LNG liquefaction and shipping capacity.

Tail Risk Event

April 20, 2020 — WTI goes negative: The May 2020 WTI futures contract expired at −$37.63/barrel. The mechanism: Cushing physical storage was near capacity (capacity utilization >90%). Short-dated futures holders had to either arrange physical delivery (impossible with no storage) or close positions by selling. The USO ETF (United States Oil Fund) held massive May contracts and was forced to roll all at once. With no buyers and physical delivery imminent, sellers accepted any price to exit. Lesson: commodity futures delivery mechanics are not theoretical when physical markets are constrained.

Gold — The Monetary Metal

Gold's price is primarily driven by: (1) Real interest rates — gold pays no yield, so its opportunity cost is the real rate; falling real rates → gold rises; (2) USD strength — gold is priced in USD; dollar weakness → gold rises (and vice versa); (3) Central bank demand — EM central banks have been net buyers since 2010 as de-dollarization theme; (4) Investment demand — ETF flows (GLD, IAU) drive short-term price action; (5) Jewelry/physical demand — India and China are the largest physical consumers.

FTX's November 2022 collapse demonstrated the uniquely dangerous combination of: (1) customer assets commingled with proprietary trading (Alameda Research) via a back-door in the accounting; (2) FTT token as "collateral" — a circular structure (Alameda held FTT, FTX was valued based on FTT, Alameda's solvency depended on FTT price); (3) CoinDesk's balance sheet leak triggered a bank run; (4) Binance's offer to acquire and immediate withdrawal created irreversible panic; (5) $8 billion hole became visible within 72 hours. The speed exceeded any traditional bank run. The lesson: in crypto, self-custody or regulated, audited custodians only.

§24 — Crypto

DeFi & Perpetual Futures

Perpetual futures are the dominant crypto derivative — larger in volume than spot in most crypto pairs. DeFi protocols replicate financial primitives (lending, options, swaps) on-chain with novel mechanisms and novel risks including smart contract exploits, oracle manipulation, and MEV extraction.

Perpetual Futures Mechanics

Perpetual futures (perps) have no expiry date. Price is kept close to spot via a funding rate mechanism: the contract continuously resets, and holders of long/short positions pay or receive funding every 8 hours (Binance, Bybit convention).

Perpetual Futures Funding Rate
Funding Rate = Clamp[(Mark Price − Index Price) / Index Price, −0.05%, 0.05%] × Premium + Base Rate

Practical formula: FR ≈ (TWAP_Perp − TWAP_Spot) / Spot × (1/8) [per 8-hour period]

Annualized funding: FR_annual = FR_8h × 3 × 365

When funding is positive: longs pay shorts → perp > spot → bearish signal (overpaying for longs)
When funding is negative: shorts pay longs → perp < spot → bullish signal (shorting expensive)
Funding rate is effectively the cost of leverage in crypto. During bull markets, BTC funding regularly reaches 50-100% annualized — making basis trades (long spot, short perp) profitable.

Liquidation Cascades

Because crypto exchanges use auto-liquidation engines (no margin calls, just immediate close of position), large concentrated positions at round-number leverage ratios create predictable liquidation clusters. When price approaches a liquidation cluster, the forced selling depresses price further, triggering the next cluster — creating a cascade. Exchanges publish liquidation heatmaps showing where concentrated positions exist; these become self-fulfilling targets for large players ("liquidation hunting").

DeFi — Automated Market Makers (AMMs)

Uniswap v2 Constant Product AMM
x × y = k (invariant)

where x, y = reserves of Token A and Token B; k = constant

Price of A in terms of B: P_A = y/x

After swap of Δx for Δy: (x + Δx)(y − Δy) = k
→ Δy = y × Δx / (x + Δx) [output for given input]

Effective Price = Δy/Δx = y/(x + Δx) [worse than spot price = price impact]

Impermanent Loss (IL) is the primary risk for AMM liquidity providers. If token A appreciates vs. token B, the AMM automatically sells A to buy B as the price moves — LPs end up holding less of the appreciating asset than if they'd simply held. IL = 2√r/(1+r) − 1 where r = price ratio change. If one token 2x's: IL ≈ 5.7%. If it 5x's: IL ≈ 42.3%. IL only "crystallizes" on withdrawal — it's a comparison to a simple hold strategy.

DeFi Risk: Oracle Manipulation

DeFi protocols rely on price oracles (Chainlink, TWAP-based on-chain prices) to determine liquidation thresholds, collateral values, and derivative payoffs. Oracle manipulation attacks exploit the gap between reported price and true price. In the March 2023 Euler Finance hack ($197M), the attacker used flash loans to inflate an asset's price on the Euler oracle, borrow against it, then repay the flash loan — extracting value that existed only in the manipulated oracle state. Flash loans make capital free within a single block, enabling attacks requiring enormous liquidity that would be impossible in traditional finance.

§25 — Risk Management

Institutional Risk Framework

Risk management is not about eliminating risk — it is about understanding, measuring, pricing, and taking the right risks in the right amounts. The full framework spans market risk, credit risk, liquidity risk, operational risk, model risk, and tail risk, each requiring distinct measurement approaches.

Market Risk Measurement

Value at Risk (VaR)

Value at Risk — Three Approaches
VaR(α, T) = maximum loss not exceeded at confidence level α over horizon T

1. Historical Simulation: sort historical P&Ls; take percentile at (1−α)
e.g., 99% 10-day VaR = 1st percentile of 1000 historical 10-day P&Ls

2. Parametric (Normal): VaR = −(μ − z_α × σ) × √T
where z_α = 2.326 for 99%, 1.645 for 95% | σ = portfolio daily vol

3. Monte Carlo: simulate thousands of scenarios; take percentile of distribution

Basel III requires 99% 10-day VaR; FRTB shifts to Expected Shortfall (ES) at 97.5%

Expected Shortfall (CVaR)

VaR tells you the threshold — not what lies beyond it. Expected Shortfall (ES), also called Conditional VaR (CVaR), is the expected loss given that loss exceeds VaR: ES = E[Loss | Loss > VaR]. ES is a coherent risk measure (unlike VaR); it is sub-additive (a portfolio's ES ≤ sum of component ES). Basel's Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) mandated ES at 97.5% replacing VaR at 99%, recognizing ES better captures tail risk.

§26 — Portfolio

Portfolio Construction

Portfolio construction translates views and constraints into a holdings structure that balances expected return, risk, diversification, and implementation cost. From Markowitz to factor models to risk parity — each framework makes different assumptions about what drives returns and how to weight them.

Mean-Variance Optimization

Markowitz Optimization Problem
Minimize: wᵀΣw [portfolio variance]
Subject to: wᵀμ ≥ target return; wᵀ1 = 1; w ≥ 0 (long-only) or unrestricted (long-short)

where: w = weight vector, Σ = covariance matrix, μ = expected return vector

Efficient Frontier: set of (μ_p, σ_p) combinations that minimize risk for each return level

Sharpe Ratio: SR = (μ_p − r_f) / σ_p [maximize for optimal risky portfolio]
The Estimation Problem

Mean-variance optimization is extremely sensitive to input assumptions, especially expected returns. Michaud (1989) showed that MVO "maximizes estimation error" — small changes in expected return assumptions produce wildly different portfolios. In practice: Black-Litterman (1990) is the institutional solution — it combines market equilibrium returns (implied by market cap weights) with investor views via Bayesian updating, producing more stable and less extreme portfolios. Robustness constraints (max weight, min weight, tracking error limits) further stabilize the solution.

Factor Models

Return attribution and portfolio construction using factor exposures:

Multi-Factor Return Model (Fama-French + Momentum)
Rᵢ − Rf = αᵢ + βᵢ,MKT × (Rm − Rf) + βᵢ,SMB × SMB + βᵢ,HML × HML + βᵢ,MOM × MOM + εᵢ

Common equity factors and their institutional interpretation:
├── Market (β): systematic exposure to equity market
├── Size (SMB): small cap premium; compensation for liquidity risk
├── Value (HML): cheap stocks vs. expensive; mean reversion premium
├── Momentum (MOM): recent winners beat recent losers; persistence premium

Risk Parity

Risk parity portfolios allocate equal risk to each asset class rather than equal capital. Since bonds have much lower volatility than equities, a 60/40 portfolio has ~90% of its risk concentrated in equities. Risk parity uses leverage on bonds to equalize contributions. The Bridgewater "All Weather" portfolio popularized this approach. Key vulnerability: when bonds and equities become positively correlated (as in 2022), risk parity loses its diversification benefit precisely when needed most.

No asset class exists in isolation. The connections between rates, equities, credit, FX, and commodities define how macro shocks transmit through the financial system — and create the cross-asset opportunities and risks that define institutional portfolio management at the highest level.

Commodity-Currency Relationships

Commodity-exporting countries' currencies are correlated with their major commodity exports: AUD and iron ore/copper, CAD and WTI crude, BRL and iron ore/soybeans, NOK and Brent crude, RUB and oil/gas. These correlations are exploitable but break down during risk-off events when all EM/commodity currencies fall together regardless of commodity prices.

The Credit-Equity Interface

Merton Model — Credit as Options
Under Merton (1974): equity = call option on firm assets (A) with strike = debt face value (D)

Equity Value = A × N(d₁) − D × e^(−rT) × N(d₂)
Debt Value (risky) = D × e^(−rT) − Put(A, D, r, σ_A, T)

Credit Spread ≈ −(1/T) × ln[N(d₂) + (A/D × e^(rT)) × N(−d₁)]

Implication: equity vol ↑ → credit spreads widen
Capital structure arbitrage: if equity implies higher leverage than CDS prices, there's a relative value trade

The USD as the Global Risk Factor

The US Dollar occupies a unique position as the global reserve currency and primary funding currency. When risk-off episodes occur: USD strengthens → EM currencies weaken → EM dollar-denominated debt becomes more expensive to service → credit conditions tighten → EM central banks raise rates or deploy FX reserves → capital outflows accelerate. This feedback loop amplifies global financial conditions beyond US borders. The Dollar Milkshake theory (Brent Johnson) argues this dynamic accelerates in a rate tightening cycle, creating dollar scarcity globally while the US economy is relatively insulated.

This transmission chain — from a volatility spike through dealer hedging to forced deleveraging — is the mechanism behind nearly every major market crash of the last 30 years. It is why VIX spikes are not just a sentiment indicator but a structural driver of equity market moves. Recognizing when dealer positioning flips (the "GEX" — gamma exposure index) from positive to negative gamma is the key to anticipating amplification vs. dampening of equity moves.

Bottom Line — Cross-Asset

The most dangerous period in financial markets is when correlations across asset classes converge to +1 during stress (everything falls together) while your portfolio was built on diversification assumptions of negative or zero correlations. Liquidity crises, margin cascades, and the USD funding squeeze combine to transmit shocks globally and simultaneously. The antidote is not more diversification within the same correlation regime — it is explicit tail hedges, regime-aware positioning, and maintaining liquidity buffers large enough to absorb margin calls without forced selling at the worst prices.
